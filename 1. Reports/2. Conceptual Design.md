# Conceptual Design Specifications

## Introduction

### Background
In 1951, Marketeer Company built the first electric golf cart [1]. Since the first golf cart’s creation, new companies have joined the scene, manufacturing electric golf carts for use on golf courses. This stems from the growing popularity of golf among both young and old people. Over the years, the use of electric golf carts has extended beyond golf courses and into residential life, universities, factories, construction sites, plants, and more. Electric carts are quiet, environmentally friendly, and cost-effective due to the absence of a gas engine [2]. However, most electric golf cars use lead-acid batteries, which have major disadvantages. Disadvantages include limited power capacities, short life cycles, and inefficient charging. These three disadvantages harm the battery, resulting in a shorter life span [3]. As a result, the consumer must throw away and buy new batteries every few years. With the constant need for golf car owners to buy new batteries and the subsequent toxic waste produced, engineers have begun to investigate environmentally friendly alternatives to powering low-power vehicles. 

### Project Description
This project aims to develop and characterize LaniEV’s **standardized retrofit kit** that converts conventional, **Club Car** electric golf cars to solar-charged, LiFePO₄ battery-powered golf cars. LaniEV is a relatively young company leading the initiative to produce solar-powered electric vehicles. LaniEV has partnered with Tennessee Technological University (TTU) to accomplish this goal. A TTU Club Car will be retrofitted with a solar-charging panel and LiFePO₄ battery provided by LaniEV. Corresponding documentation, analysis, and characterization of the conversion will support Lani's commercialization of these retrofit kits. During the retrofitting of the golf car, challenges may arise from the pre-existing condition of the golf car, as it was modified by a previous capstone project. The project will also analyze the effectiveness of Lani's retrofit kit compared to existing solutions. Additionally, the project aims to develop a **universal conversion kit** that applies to all three name-brand golf car manufacturers (Club Car, E-Z-GO, and Yamaha). 

The documentation provided by this capstone project will provide LaniEV with the accreditation needed to market its solutions. Lani is not only looking at privately owned golf cars but also entire organizations and companies to convert to solar power. One of the main engineering challenges for creating a universal conversion kit is that golf car manufacturers purposefully design golf cars with a unique structure to distance themselves from competitors [4]. Because the retrofitting of the entire frame of the cars heavily depends on the existing structure of the vehicle, it may be difficult to engineer a *one-size-fits-all* product.

## Restating the Fully Formulated Problem 

### Objective
This project's primary customer is Lani Solar, LLC (LaniEV). LaniEV has developed a standardized retrofit kit for existing models of electric Club Car golf cars and is working to develop a universal conversion kit for low-speed electric vehicles (LSVs), personal transport vehicles (PVTs), and golf cars. The Club Car retrofit kit and the universal conversion kits share the function of transforming legacy lead-acid battery systems to LiFePO₄ (Lithium Iron Phosphate) battery banks integrated with self-charging roof solar panels. The goal is to commercialize the conversion kits for entire fleet conversions. Secondary stakeholders include Tennessee Technological University (TTU), as the owner of the control golf car used for the project, and as the ensuring agency for documented safety, reliability, and performance data. 

Legacy golf cars continue to utilize lead-acid battery systems due to their well-documented electrical performance and broad availability. These systems present constraints such as low energy density, volume constraints, short lifecycle, load limits, maintenance requirements, and environmental concerns. These limitations increase cost and decrease sustainability for large-scale applications. Alternative power systems, such as LiFePO₄ batteries integrated with solar charging, could potentially address the limitations inherent in the lead-acid batteries. The growing interest in alternative systems demands formal documentation for conversion processes. The lack of validation prevents large-scale migration of LSV, PVT, and golf car platforms to alternative energy systems.
 
The primary objective of this project is to develop a universal solar panel attachment kit. The attachment kit is intended to be used on any of the three leading golf car manufacturers. This project will also address the lack of technical validation through meticulous examination and documentation of the retrofit process, and will also establish a validated framework for implementing the standardized retrofit kit. The development includes initial state characterization of the existing lead-acid system, formalization of the conversion methodology, post-conversion performance evaluation, and a quantified analysis of environmental and economic impact. The Tennessee Tech University Club Car will serve as the control platform for this process. Baseline electrical and functional characteristics of the legacy system shall be documented prior to conversion. Following installation of the LiFePO₄ battery and solar charging systems, the vehicle shall undergo structured testing to quantify energy efficiency, power delivery, charging behavior, and operational performance. The resulting documentation shall provide the necessary metrics required for the retrofit kit to be commercialized.

### Constraints
- **NEC Article 690**
    - **Origin:** The National Electrical Code
    - **Impact:** Sets standards for solar photovoltaic systems and applies to the electrical integration of the solar panel, charge controller, and associated wiring. 

- **29 CFR 1910**
    - **Origin:** OSHA Regulations
    - **Impact:** Governs safe practices during live testing and assembly to protect team members working with electrical systems, including lockout/tagout (1910.147) and electrical safety-related work practices (1910 Subpart S).

- **UL Certification**
    - **Origin:** Underwriters Laboratories standards
    - **Impact:** Components such as charge controllers, DC-DC converters, battery management systems (BMS), and wiring assemblies may require compliance with relevant UL standards (e.g., UL 1741 for inverters and controllers, UL 1973 for stationary batteries, UL 2580 for electric vehicle battery systems). Use of UL-listed components reduces liability exposure and supports insurability.

- **SAE Standards (J2344, J1772)**
    - **Origin:** SAE International
    - **Impact:** Provides guidelines for EV battery system safety (J2344) and conductive charging interfaces (J1772), ensuring predictable and safe operation under environmental and electrical conditions.

- **IEEE Standards (1547, grounding, insulation coordination)**
    - **Origin:** Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers
    - **Impact:** Governs grounding, insulation, and interconnection of distributed energy resources to ensure safe integration with electrical systems and prevent faults.

- **NFPA 70 / 70E / 855**
    - **Origin:** National Fire Protection Association
    - **Impact:** NFPA 70 (NEC) governs electrical installations. NFPA 70E addresses electrical safety in the workplace and may apply during testing and live diagnostics. NFPA 855 may apply if energy storage systems exceed certain capacity thresholds.

- **FMVSS (Low-Speed Vehicle Standards)**
    - **Origin:** Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards
    - **Impact:** Governs lighting, braking, and electrical safety integration for LSVs intended for on-road use, ensuring safe operation under applicable vehicle regulations.

- **EPA and Environmental Regulations**
    - **Origin:** Environmental Protection Agency and state-level rules
    - **Impact:** Requires compliant battery disposal, recycling, and hazardous material handling for lithium-based battery systems.

- **ANSI Standards (Z535, electrical safety labeling)**
    - **Origin:** American National Standards Institute
    - **Impact:** Governs electrical safety labeling, signage, and equipment performance to ensure clear hazard communication and standardization of high-voltage warnings.



## Comparative Analysis of Potential Solutions

### **Golf Car Power Solutions**

#### **Gas**
Gas-powered golf cars are fueled by gasoline using a combustion engine. While gas-powered cars are powerful and have a long range, they produce direct emissions, require regular maintenance, and are louder than electric alternatives [5]. This traditional solution is becoming less and less popular and increasingly concerning for environmental scientists [6]. Gas power is not a suitable solution for the goals of this project.

#### **Traditional Electric**
Traditional electric golf cars are the most popular cars in the golfing industry because they are easy to recharge and low-maintenance. The electric golf car runs on a battery pack, typically lead-acid or lithium-ion, that is charged by plugging into a standard electrical outlet. They produce zero direct emissions and are quieter than gas-powered golf cars, making them the preferred choice for most golf courses and residential use [5], [7]. However, traditional electric golf cars are dependent on the power grid for charging, which limits their sustainability and independence from conventional energy sources.

#### **Renewable**
Renewable energy-powered golf cars use natural energy sources to charge or directly power the car. The most practical renewable solution for a golf car is solar energy, delivered through solar panels mounted on the roof of the car. Solar panels convert sunlight into electricity using the photovoltaic effect, which is then used to charge the battery pack and power the car. This solution eliminates dependence on the power grid, produces zero direct emissions, and has lower long-term operating costs compared to both gas and traditional electric options [8].
There are different kinds of solar panels on the market today. The most common are monocrystalline, polycrystalline, and thin-film solar panels. Each type was evaluated based on the following design considerations: dimensions, weight, cost, lifespan, efficiency, and durability. Additionally, each panel type was assessed based on the following influencing factors: where it is produced, cost, and environmental impact [9].

### **Solar Panel Analysis**
---

**Monocrystalline Solar Panels**

The first monocrystalline solar cell was invented at Bell Labs by Daryl Chapin, Calvin Fuller, and Gerald Pearson with a 6% efficiency rate. This efficiency rate increased over time, reaching approximately 17–24% in modern panels. Monocrystalline solar cells are made from a single-crystal silicon wafer grown using the Czochralski process [10].

|---------|---------------|------|

| Weight | 44–50 lbs. | Adds weight to golf car roof |

---

**Influencing Factors**

#### Manufacturing Location 
Monocrystalline panels are primarily produced in China, with the largest manufacturers being LONGi Green Energy, JinkoSolar, and Trina Solar. This concentration of production in a single country can affect supply chain reliability and lead times.

#### Cost 
Typical cost ranges from **$280–$440** for a 400W panel, making the upfront cost the highest among panel types. However, their longer lifespan and higher efficiency can offset this cost over time, making them a strong long-term investment.

#### Environmental Impact  
The manufacturing process is energy-intensive due to high temperatures and the need for extremely pure silicon. However, once installed, the panels produce clean energy with zero direct emissions and have the longest lifespan of the three types, reducing the frequency of replacement and waste.

---

**Polycrystalline Solar Panels**

Polycrystalline solar panels were developed in 1981 as a cheaper alternative to monocrystalline panels. Instead of using a single crystal of silicon, polycrystalline panels are made by melting multiple silicon fragments together, which gives them their characteristic blue, speckled appearance [11].

<figcaption><strong>Table 2.</strong> Design Specifications of Polycrystalline Solar Panels.</figcaption>

| Property | Typical Value | Notes |
|---------|---------------|------|
| Dimensions | ~79 × 39 in | Standard 400W panel |
| Weight | 44–50 lbs. | Similar to monocrystalline panels |
| Lifespan | 25–35 years | Slightly shorter than monocrystalline |
| Efficiency | 15–17% | Lower efficiency |
| Durability | Moderate–High | Slightly reduced performance in high heat |

---

**Influencing Factors**

#### Manufacturing Location  
Like monocrystalline panels, polycrystalline panels are predominantly manufactured in China, with some production in other Asian countries such as Malaysia and Vietnam. This makes them subject to similar supply chain considerations as monocrystalline panels.

#### Cost  
Typical cost ranges from **$200–$320** for a 400W panel. Polycrystalline panels are the most affordable silicon-based option, making them attractive for projects with tighter budgets. However, their lower efficiency means more panels may be needed to achieve the same power output

#### Environmental Impact  
Polycrystalline panels have a slightly lower environmental impact during manufacturing compared to monocrystalline panels because the crystal growth process is simpler and less energy-intensive. They still produce clean energy during operation, though their shorter lifespan means they may need to be replaced sooner, generating more waste over time.

---

**Thin-Film Solar Panels (CIGS)**

CIGS (Copper Indium Gallium Selenide) solar panels use a different approach than silicon-based panels. Instead of cutting wafers from a silicon crystal, the semiconductor material is deposited directly onto a substrate in very thin layers. First developed in 1974, CIGS panels are the fastest to manufacture of the three panel types, typically taking only 1–2 days to produce [12].

<figcaption><strong>Table 3.</strong> Design Specifications of Thin-Film (CIGS) Solar Panels.</figcaption>

| Property | Typical Value | Notes |
|---------|---------------|------|
| Dimensions | Custom sizes | Flexible form factor |
| Weight | 5–7 lbs. | Much lighter than silicon panels |
| Lifespan | 10–20 years | Shortest lifespan |
| Efficiency | 12–14% | Lowest efficiency |
| Durability | Moderate | Performs well in heat and low light |

---

**Influencing Factors**

#### Manufacturing Location  
CIGS panels are produced by a more geographically diverse group of manufacturers compared to silicon-based panels, including companies in the United States, Germany, and Japan. This wider distribution can provide greater supply chain flexibility and potentially shorter lead times depending on sourcing location.

#### Cost  
Typical cost ranges from **$240–$400** for a 400W panel. While this places CIGS panels in the middle of the price range, their lower efficiency and shorter lifespan generally make them less cost-effective over the long term compared to silicon-based panels.

#### Environmental Impact  
CIGS panels present additional environmental considerations due to the use of indium and gallium, which are relatively rare materials, and the presence of small amounts of cadmium in some variants, which is toxic. Although the manufacturing process is less energy-intensive than silicon panel production, the use of rare and potentially hazardous materials is an important consideration for environmentally focused projects.

#### **Solution**

Based on the design considerations and influencing factors outlined above, the monocrystalline solar panel was selected as the best solution for powering the solar golf car. Gas-powered golf cars were eliminated due to their direct emissions and maintenance requirements. Traditional electric golf cars were eliminated due to their dependence on the power grid for charging. The solar-powered solution using monocrystalline panels is the best fit for this project because of its superior efficiency (17-24%), lifespan (25-40 years), and durability. Additionally, monocrystalline panels are the most environmentally responsible choice. Although monocrystalline panels cost more upfront, in the long term, they are the best option.


### **Battery Analysis**

#### **FLA (Flooded Lead-Acid)**

The lead-acid battery was invented by French physician Gaston Planté in 1859. At the end of the 19th century, electric cars appeared on the roads powered mostly by lead-acid batteries. The late 1960s saw improvements with casings making them thinner and lighting providing the use of more active material at no sacrifice of weight or volume. Despite being over 165 years old, FLA batteries remain widely used in golf carts due to their low cost and reliability. Each battery cell contains a series of lead dioxide (PbO₂) plates as the positive electrode and sponge lead (Pb) plates as the negative electrode, submerged in an electrolyte solution. That electrolyte is a 35% sulfuric acid and 65% water solution, which allows for the chemical reaction needed to release electrons. Lead-acid batteries also have a high recycling rate of around 98%, this recycling rate alleviate concerns about the toxicity of their materials and the environmental impact [22].

#### **AGM (Absorbed Glass Mat)**

AGM technology was originally invented in 1972. AGM batteries were initially used in military aircraft because they were powerful, durable, and safe. They were later used as backup batteries for call and data centers, and eventually found their way into golf carts, marine vessels, RVs, and consumer vehicles. AGM batteries share the same basic chemistry as FLA batteries, with the difference being that, instead of a liquid, the electrolyte solution is absorbed into mats of finely spun glass fibers. The mats soak up the solution like a sponge and are then sandwiched between the lead plates, creating a stable connection between the electrolyte and the plates [23].

#### **Nickel-Cadmium (NiCd)**

NiCd batteries were invented by Swedish chemist Waldemar Jungner in 1899 as an alternative to lead-acid batteries. They are constructed from four key components: a nickel oxyhydroxide cathode, a metallic cadmium anode, a 30% potassium hydroxide electrolyte solution, and a porous polymer separator between the plates. During discharge, the cadmium anode is oxidized, and the nickel oxyhydroxide cathode is reduced, releasing electrical energy. During charging, the reaction is completely reversed, restoring the battery to its original state [24].

#### **Lithium-Ion (LiFePO₄)**

LiFePO₄ batteries are composed of four key components: a **cathode** (positive electrode) made of lithium iron phosphate coated on aluminum foil; an **anode** made of graphite layered on copper foil; a thin polymer **separator** that physically separates the cathode and anode while allowing lithium ions to pass through; and an **electrolyte** composed of solvents, lithium salts, and additives that enable ionic transfer. A built-in Battery Management System (BMS) monitors each cell, preventing overcharging, over-discharging, and overheating [25].

#### **Solution**

Based on the design considerations and influencing factors outlined above, the Lithium Ion (LiFePO₄) battery was selected as the best solution for powering the solar golf car. FLA batteries were eliminated due to their high maintenance requirements, heavy weight, and short range. AGM batteries were eliminated due to their weight and shorter lifespan relative to lithium. NiCd batteries were eliminated due to their high cost, environmental concerns related to toxic cadmium, and extremely limited use in golf cart applications. The LiFePO₄ solution is the best fit for this project because of its superior range (40–70 miles), lifespan (10 years / 5,000–6,000 cycles), low maintenance, and lightest weight of all options at just 86 lbs. Although LiFePO₄ batteries have a higher upfront cost than lead acid alternatives, their longer lifespan and lower maintenance make them the most cost-effective and environmentally responsible long-term solution.

## High-Level Solution

### Maximizing Stakeholder Goal Attainment
Stakeholders' goals are maximized through a structured, documentation-driven approach. Documentation detailing the engineering process of accomplishing the established objectives (see the *Specifications* section under *Restating the Fully Formulated Problem*), will serve as the basis for the deliverables. The documents eventually provided to LaniEV will collectively provide a technical foundation required for marketing their conversion kits.

### Established Constraints 
Risk is minimized by adhering to applicable regulatory standards throughout the design process. Electrical integration follows NEC Article 690 for solar photovoltaic systems, and component selection prioritizes UL-listed hardware where applicable. OSHA safety practices govern all live testing and assembly work. SAE and IEEE standards inform the electrical integration and grounding of the system. This approach reduces liability exposure and supports the long-term insurability and safety of the product. 

### Resource Utilization
Resource utilization is optimized by leveraging the existing Club Car as the test platform. LaniEV supplies the primary retrofit components, including the LiFePO₄ battery and solar panel, which allows the team's budget to focus on instrumentation, replacement parts, and testing equipment.

## Atomic Subsystem Specifications

### Vehicle Preparation
Responsible for preparing the existing golf car for retrofit. Remove existing wires, the steering system implemented by the previous team, and the existing structural mounts. The control golf car shall be fully prepared for the implementation of other subsystems. This is the first step in the conversion of legacy electric-powered golf cars to solar-powered golf cars.

### Battery System Installation

## Ethical, Professional, and Standards Considerations

The development of a solar conversion kit presents an affordable and efficient method for consumers to convert existing electrically powered golf cars into golf cars that run completely on solar energy. This section will evaluate the project's ethical and professional implications, as well as the responsibilities and standards that must be adhered to. This section will also detail the project's broader implications on society, the environment, public health and safety, as well as the economy. These considerations have influenced the conceptual design of this project, as the design takes into account the constraints and regulations necessary to ensure a safe retrofit kit that is of excellent quality.

### Ethical Discussions

There are several glaring ethical issues that may arise during the project's lifespan. The first is to ensure that the customer completely understands both the capabilities and shortcomings of the retrofit kit. All constraints that the retrofit kit possesses must be properly communicated. Data that has been collected during the course of this project, including battery life, braking and steering capabilities, and propulsion power, must be accurately and effectively communicated to the customer to avoid confusion around the performance of the finished product. 

The safety of those who use the retrofit kit in all circumstances must also be remembered. All modifications to the existing golf car, using the retrofit kit, must comply with ANSI/OPEI safety and performance requirements for golf cars [38]. Additionally, it is imperative that the installation process of the retrofit kit is also deemed safe, in order to ensure those who are working to implement these retrofit kits are kept safe.

### Broader Societal Impacts

The impacts that a retrofit kit would have on society as a whole are mostly positive, bringing an affordable and efficient method to convert an existing battery-powered club car to solar power, with some negatives. 

#### Cultural and Societal Impacts
The use of golf cars in the modern era can be seen everywhere. College campuses, resorts, retirement communities, and industrial sites all use golf cars in one way or another. A retrofit kit to convert these existing golf cars to be powered by solar energy would usher in a new way for electric vehicle technology to be used. This would allow the world to be more exposed, in a positive way, to both solar and electric vehicle technologies. Intellectual innovations regarding both solar and electric vehicle technologies would follow, allowing for improved designs and products in these areas of study. 

#### Environmental Impacts
A solar-powered conversion kit would have both positive and negative impacts on the environment. Positives include reduced reliance on grid charging stations for battery-powered electric vehicles. Battery life would also be extended for such vehicles, assuming ideal weather conditions. Carbon emissions on a local scale would be reduced, as there would be fewer gas-powered vehicles in use, as well. Negative environmental impacts would include the emissions created from the increased production of solar panels in use by such retrofit kits. Extracting materials necessary for the production of these conversion kits would also create increased emissions on the environment. End-of-life disposal of batteries being used by these solar-powered vehicles is a concern. It is paramount that used batteries be disposed of in a proper and professional manner. 

### Economic Impacts
Converting an already existing battery-powered golf car to be solar-powered would be cheaper than the alternative of purchasing an entirely new golf car. For companies looking to mass-convert existing golf cars, it would also be an efficient and cost-effective way to convert large numbers of golf cars in a short period of time. Such a retrofit kit would also create a large number of jobs, as there would be many more tasks that would need to be fulfilled to achieve the successful implementation of these kits. These jobs would appear all over the economy. Jobs would be created to gather the resources necessary for the production of these retrofit kits, as well as jobs in manufacturing, in order to create more things that the kit would possess, such as solar panels, solar-rechargeable batteries, arms to hold said solar panel, and any extra wiring needed for the implementation of the new battery. There would also be an increase in skilled labor, as jobs must be created to both install and maintain these retrofit kits.

#### Public Safety Impacts
Safety is of the utmost importance in a project such as this, which requires potentially fatal amounts of electricity to operate. All constraints and specifications listed in the **Fully Formulated Problem** section must be followed to ensure the safety of all involved in the design, implementation, and consumption process of this retrofit kit.

#### Public Health Impacts
Carbon emissions would be reduced on a local scale by a considerable amount for consumers looking to convert fleets of golf cars to solar-powered ones. These reduced emissions would have a positive effect on the community, creating cleaner air for inhabitants. On a cognitive level, the development of a retrofit kit could potentially inspire increased research and innovations on technologies involved in the retrofit kit system.

### Influences on Design

Thorough research has been conducted to better understand certain components to be used in such a retrofit kit, and the decision has been made to use a monocrystalline solar panel and a lithium-ion (LiFePO₄) battery for the retrofit kit. The monocrystalline panel was chosen primarily because of its higher efficiency when compared to other types of panels. It also has a longer lifespan and greater durability, which will all contribute to a higher quality golf car. Monocrystalline panels are also better for the environment, as they have zero direct emissions after their manufacturing. Lithium-ion batteries were also chosen to be used in this retrofit kit for several reasons. They have superior range, their lifespans are much longer, they are the lowest maintenance, and they are the lightest weight when compared to the other battery options.
